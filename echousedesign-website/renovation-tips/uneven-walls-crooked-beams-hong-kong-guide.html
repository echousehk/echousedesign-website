Uneven Walls and Crooked Beams in Hong Kong Flats: What to Check, Who Is Responsible and How to Respond

An uneven wall or a visibly sloping beam can be a finishing issue, an existing building condition, or a sign that the wrong question is being asked. The key is to identify the condition early, record it clearly and never treat a structural element as something to cut or conceal without proper advice.

Featured image supplied by ECHOUSE — Renovation Inspection: Who Is Responsible for Wall and Beam Quality?

Few discoveries are more frustrating than noticing a wall, tile line or beam that does not look straight after the work is nearly complete. A shadow may reveal a wave in a painted wall. A cabinet may sit neatly while the beam above it appears to slope. Tile joints may look uneven at eye level even though the room felt acceptable during earlier site visits.

For a homeowner, the immediate question is often: Who is responsible? The more useful first question is: What exactly is not straight, how was it measured, and what was agreed in the scope? The answer can differ dramatically between an existing building condition, a finishing tolerance, a missing levelling step and a structural element that must not be altered casually.

This guide explains how to approach the issue calmly, what to look for before finishes hide the substrate, and how to have a better conversation with the renovation team. It is written for Hong Kong flat owners, residents and landlords who want to protect both the finished appearance and the long-term safety of their home.

1. Separate the Existing Structure From the Finished Surface

An older flat may already have walls, floors or beams that are not perfectly level or plumb. That fact does not automatically make a poor finished result acceptable, but it does change the technical and commercial conversation. A painter’s skim coat, for example, is designed to refine a surface; it is not necessarily a solution for a substantial existing deviation in the building fabric.

Begin by identifying the layer you are looking at. Is the issue visible in paint, plaster, tile, cabinetry, a false ceiling or the beam itself? Is the line consistently off, or does it change across the wall? Was the existing condition visible before work started? Photographs, measurement notes, drawings and site records can be more helpful than memory once several trades have worked in the space.

A renovation team should raise a significant pre-existing condition early, explain the options and clarify whether levelling, replastering, a false wall or another treatment is included in the scope. A homeowner, in turn, should ask for the recommended solution and any visual compromise to be documented before the finish is selected. This prevents the common problem of discovering a limitation only when paint, tiles or custom joinery are already in place.

2. Know What a Finish Coat Can—and Cannot—Do

Paint, plaster and decorative finishes can improve the appearance of minor surface imperfections, but they should not be expected to correct every underlying condition. If a wall is materially out of level, a thin finishing layer may follow the same profile rather than transform it into a perfectly straight plane. Under strong side lighting, even small variations can become more visible.

This is why early inspection matters. Before tiling, painting or fitting joinery, ask the contractor how the wall will be checked and what standard is being targeted. If the room includes large-format tiles, a long run of cabinetry, a mirror, a glass partition or a linear light, the surrounding surfaces may need more careful preparation because those elements make alignment easier to judge.

Do not rely on an informal glance alone. Ask for a practical explanation of the issue, the proposed treatment and its likely visual effect. If a more extensive levelling process is recommended, understand the effect it may have on room dimensions, opening widths, skirting details, door frames and the budget. The best solution is not always the one that makes a single line look perfectly straight; it is the one that resolves the room coherently and safely.

3. Tile Lines and Joinery Reveal Preparation Quality

Tiles, shadow gaps, cabinet edges and long horizontal lines act like visual rulers. They can make a wall that looked acceptable in bare concrete or plaster suddenly appear uneven. This does not necessarily mean the tile installer or joiner created the original problem, but it does mean the interfaces between trades should have been considered before installation.

Where a tiled wall is involved, ask how the substrate was prepared, whether the layout was set from a level reference and how corners, joints and cuts were planned. With cabinetry, consider whether the furniture needs to follow a wall, a ceiling line or a deliberately level datum. In some rooms, a small visual adjustment in the joinery design can make an existing building imperfection much less noticeable. In others, a more fundamental correction may be necessary before fabrication begins.

The point is not to demand a cosmetic solution after the fact. It is to make sure the chosen solution matches the nature of the issue. Replacing a finish without correcting an underlying preparation problem can create unnecessary disruption without producing a better result.

4. Treat Beams as a Safety Question Before a Design Question

A beam is not simply a line to be made visually neater. If it is part of the building structure, it must never be cut, removed or altered as though it were an ordinary partition or decorative bulkhead. The Hong Kong Government states that work involving structural walls, beams or columns requires an owner to appoint an Authorised Person and a Registered Structural Engineer to assess feasibility and submit plans to the Buildings Department; the work should proceed through the appropriate approval and contractor process.1

This is an important distinction in a renovation conversation. A designer may suggest a false ceiling, a trim line, a cabinet return or a lighting detail that improves the visual relationship around an existing beam. Those are design approaches to the space around the beam. They are not permission to alter the beam itself.

If a beam appears unusually uneven, cracked, damaged or inconsistent with drawings you have been given, pause the cosmetic discussion. Ask for a qualified professional assessment rather than requesting a quick site adjustment. In a compact flat, a false ceiling or carefully designed edge can often improve the perception of the room, but safety and compliance must come first.

5. Choose Remedies That Match the Cause

Once the condition is understood, there may be several ways forward. The right option depends on the size of the deviation, the finish that has already been installed, the room dimensions, the building condition and the agreed scope of work.

For a modest wall-surface issue, additional preparation and refinishing may be appropriate. Where a greater visual correction is needed, a designed lining or false wall can sometimes create a true new plane, although it will use some floor area and must be coordinated with electrical points, skirting, doors and adjacent joinery. Around a non-alterable beam, a false ceiling or carefully proportioned trim can sometimes bring the visual line of the room into balance.

For tiled areas, the remedy requires particular care. Removing tiles may affect underlying finishes and may be disruptive, especially where waterproofing systems or services are involved. A contractor should explain what can be corrected, what must be protected and what the likely visual outcome will be before demolition begins. Avoid accepting a vague promise that an issue will simply be “covered.” Ask what is being covered, why that approach is appropriate and how it will be detailed.

6. Use a Clear Conversation and Handover Checklist

A productive discussion is specific. Instead of saying “the wall looks wrong,” identify the location, the visible condition, the viewing angle, the finish affected and the remedy you are asking the team to explain. Photograph the issue in daylight and under the room’s planned lighting. Mark the location on a drawing if there are several similar areas.

Before accepting a proposed correction, ask these questions:

  • What is the existing condition and how was it measured?
  • Is the issue in the structure, the base preparation, the finish or the alignment with another element?
  • Was any correction included in the original scope, and if not, what would it involve?
  • What effect will the proposed remedy have on space, services, access panels, door frames or other finishes?
  • Is any professional assessment needed before the work continues?
  • What will the finished visual standard look like, and how will it be checked at handover?

At final inspection, look at key walls from the normal positions where you will use the room, not only from directly in front of the affected area. Check long tile lines, cabinet edges, ceiling junctions, mirrors and strong lighting angles. Make a list of agreed defects or touch-ups, then keep written records of the proposed completion steps.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is an uneven wall always the contractor’s fault?

No. The underlying building may already have an uneven condition, while the scope may or may not include correction work. The important question is whether the condition was identified, explained and handled appropriately before finishes were installed.

Can a painter make a significantly uneven wall perfectly straight?

Not necessarily. A finishing coat is not a universal levelling solution. The appropriate treatment depends on the degree and cause of the deviation, the final finish and the space available for a more substantial correction.

Can I ask a contractor to cut or reduce a beam that looks crooked?

Do not treat a beam as an ordinary finishing item. If the beam may be structural, seek qualified professional advice. Alteration of structural walls, beams or columns is subject to specific Hong Kong requirements.1

Is a false ceiling always the answer to an uneven beam?

No. It can be one design approach where appropriate, but it should not hide a condition that needs professional assessment. The solution must be coordinated with headroom, services, lighting and the building’s requirements.

What should I record during a handover inspection?

Record the location, photograph the condition, note the affected finish, identify the agreed remedy and confirm who will complete it and by when. Clear records help avoid misunderstandings between trades and homeowners.

Resolve the Condition Before It Becomes an Argument

A straight-looking room begins with early communication, not a last-minute cosmetic fix. Understand the existing condition, ask the renovation team to explain the preparation strategy, and keep structural safety separate from visual preference. When an issue is raised early and documented clearly, there is more room to choose a sensible remedy that protects both the home and the finished result.
